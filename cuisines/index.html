Where New York Eats

Every restaurant in the city has to tell the Health Department what it serves. Map those answers and the city's immigrant neighborhoods appear, block by block.

Data as of … · … restaurants · … cuisines

Most of New York's restaurants serve something you can find in any neighborhood: American, pizza, coffee, Chinese takeout. Those tell you little about a place, so this map sets them aside and asks a different question: which cuisine is far more common here than in the city as a whole?

Each hexagon below covers about a quarter mile. Its color is the family of the cuisine that is most over-represented in it, and darker means a stronger standout. A block where 1 in 3 restaurants is Filipino, when citywide it is 1 in 700, is very dark.

The whole city

Before zooming in, the pattern is already visible: orange South Asian in central Queens, purple Eastern European and kosher in south Brooklyn, pink Caribbean across central Brooklyn and southeast Queens, yellow Latin American along the 7 train and in upper Manhattan.

Gray hexagons have restaurants, but none of them stand out.

Little Manila, Woodside

Twelve of the city's 37 Filipino restaurants are in Woodside, most of them along Roosevelt Avenue under the 7 train. That is about 60 times the citywide rate, the most concentrated enclave in the data.

Bangladeshi Queens, and beyond

Jackson Heights has 15 Bangladeshi restaurants, Briarwood–Jamaica Hills 14, Jamaica 8. The community has spread too: smaller clusters show up in Kensington, Brooklyn, in Cypress Hills, and in Parkchester in the Bronx.

Richmond Hill and the eastern edge

Richmond Hill has more Indian restaurants than any other neighborhood (21), many of them Indo-Caribbean, sitting next to Guyanese and Trinidadian spots that the city files as Caribbean. Further east, Glen Oaks and Bellerose at the Nassau line have a second Indian cluster.

Two Koreatowns

Midtown's Koreatown, around 32nd Street, has 97 Korean restaurants. Flushing's Murray Hill, around the Long Island Rail Road station, has 88. Together they hold almost half of the city's Korean restaurants. A third, smaller cluster sits in Bayside.

Hell's Kitchen is Thai

No neighborhood has more Thai restaurants than Hell's Kitchen: 15 of them, with Chelsea and Midtown close behind. That is Manhattan's restaurant-dense version of Elmhurst, Queens, where 12 Thai restaurants anchor the city's Thai community.

Little Brazil on West 46th Street has mostly faded: Midtown has 3 Brazilian restaurants, and Astoria now has the most, with 6.

Astoria: Greek, Egyptian, Brazilian

Steinway, north Astoria, has 17 Greek restaurants. Along Steinway Street, “Little Egypt” helps give Astoria 4 of the city's 12 Egyptian restaurants, among Moroccan and Middle Eastern places.

Greenpoint, still Polish

Gentrification has changed Greenpoint, but 8 of the city's 18 Polish restaurants remain there. That is 45 times what you would expect.

Brighton Beach

Brighton Beach, Sheepshead Bay and Homecrest together have 41 of the city's 127 Eastern European restaurants and 23 of its 42 Russian ones.

Kosher Brooklyn and Queens

Borough Park (41), Midwood (39) and Homecrest (33) hold a third of the city's kosher restaurants. Smaller clusters mark the Orthodox communities of Williamsburg, Forest Hills, and Kew Gardens Hills.

The Caribbean belt

The city's biggest enclave by area is Caribbean: from Crown Heights through East Flatbush in Brooklyn, then in southeast Queens from South Ozone Park, which has the most Caribbean restaurants (56), out to Laurelton. In the north Bronx, Wakefield has Caribbean spots alongside the area's Irish pubs.

Adding Chinese back in: three Chinatowns

We left Chinese out of the map because there is a Chinese restaurant in almost every neighborhood. Add it back and three Chinatowns appear. Flushing (272) and Sunset Park (150) now each have more Chinese restaurants than Manhattan's Chinatown (122), and Bensonhurst (68) is a fourth.

And, since we have it: the letter grades

The same dataset is where the Health Department's letter grades come from. Overall, … of graded restaurants have an A. By cuisine, each line runs from the citywide rate to that cuisine's share of A's. The gaps are real but small, and may reflect where and how restaurants are inspected as much as how clean the kitchens are.

About the data

Source. DOHMH New York City Restaurant Inspection Results, one row per restaurant, limited to the … that are open, have been inspected, have a cuisine on file, and have a map location. Neighborhoods are the city's 2010 Neighborhood Tabulation Areas.

What's missing. Food carts and trucks, and most bodegas and groceries, which the state inspects, are not in this dataset. Each restaurant picks one cuisine from a fixed list of about 90 labels, so “African” covers Senegalese and Nigerian alike, and there is no Uzbek, Tibetan or Dominican label. “Spanish” is how many Dominican and Puerto Rican restaurants describe themselves.

The math. For each hexagon and cuisine, expected is the number of restaurants in the hexagon times the cuisine's citywide share. A cuisine stands out when it has at least two restaurants there and at least 1.5 times the expected number. Among those, the hexagon shows the one that is furthest above expected, scored by Poisson deviance (k·ln(k/e) − (k − e)). That score weighs a big excess over a small one, so three Ethiopian restaurants don't beat twenty kosher ones just because Ethiopian is rarer.

Colors group cuisines into eight regional families. The map labels each cluster with its cuisine name, so you don't have to tell colors apart.
