Where New York Eats

Every restaurant in the city has to tell the Health Department what it serves. Map those answers and the city's immigrant neighborhoods appear, block by block.

Data as of … · … restaurants · … cuisines

Every dot is one of the city's … restaurants, colored by the cuisine's region of the world. A dot is bright when its cuisine is far more common on that block than across the city. Gray dots are the everyday places: American, pizza, coffee and, for now, Chinese.

Every restaurant in New York

The bright clusters are enclaves: orange South Asian in Queens, purple kosher and Russian in south Brooklyn, pink Caribbean across Brooklyn and southeast Queens.

South Asian Queens

Midtown is full of Indian restaurants, but the enclaves are in Queens. Jackson Heights (15) and Briarwood–Jamaica Hills (14) lead in Bangladeshi, and Richmond Hill in Indian (21).

Little Manila

Twelve of the city's 37 Filipino restaurants sit along Roosevelt Avenue in Woodside, about 60 times the citywide rate.

South Brooklyn

Borough Park and Midwood have 80 kosher restaurants between them. Further south, Brighton Beach and Sheepshead Bay are Russian and Eastern European.

The Caribbean belt

From Crown Heights to East Flatbush, then southeast Queens, where South Ozone Park has the most Caribbean restaurants in the city (56).

Three Chinatowns

Add Chinese back in. Flushing (272) and Sunset Park (150) both have more Chinese restaurants than Manhattan's Chinatown (122).

About the data

Source. DOHMH New York City Restaurant Inspection Results, one row per restaurant, limited to the … that are open, have been inspected, have a cuisine on file, and have a map location. Neighborhoods are the city's 2010 Neighborhood Tabulation Areas.

What's missing. Food carts and trucks, and most bodegas and groceries, which the state inspects, are not in this dataset. Each restaurant picks one cuisine from a fixed list of about 90 labels, so “African” covers Senegalese and Nigerian alike, and there is no Uzbek, Tibetan or Dominican label. “Spanish” is how many Dominican and Puerto Rican restaurants describe themselves.

The math. The city is divided into hexagons about 200 meters across (roughly two avenue blocks). For each hexagon and cuisine, expected is the number of restaurants in the hexagon times the cuisine's citywide share. A restaurant's dot is bright when its own cuisine stands out in its hexagon. Labels apply the same test to quarter-mile hexagons. A cuisine stands out when it has at least two restaurants there and at least 1.5 times the expected number. The hexagon view and the labels show the one furthest above expected, scored by Poisson deviance (k·ln(k/e) − (k − e)). That score weighs a big excess over a small one, so three Ethiopian restaurants don't beat twenty kosher ones just because Ethiopian is rarer.

Colors group cuisines into eight regional families. The map labels each cluster with its cuisine name, so you don't have to tell colors apart.
